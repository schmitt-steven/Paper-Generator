# Benford's Law Validation of German Zensus 2022: A Dual-Metric MAD and Chi-Square Framework for Distinguishing Structural Anomalies from Sample-Size Artifacts

## Abstract

Standard statistical tests often flag trivial deviations as anomalies in large census datasets, leading to false positives that obscure genuine data quality issues. This study addresses this limitation by applying a dual-metric validation strategy combining Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) and first-digit frequency analysis to German municipal census data from Zensus 2022. Results show that raw population counts and area measurements align closely with Benford's Law expectations, exhibiting strong alignment with minimal deviation scores. In contrast, synthetic datasets designed to mimic uniform randomness or human fabrication bias displayed significant deviations, confirming the method's ability to distinguish non-natural patterns from statistical noise. Although derived density ratios also conformed to expected distributions, they showed slightly higher variation than base variables, suggesting minor calculation-related noise without compromising overall validity. This approach successfully separates genuine structural anomalies from sample-size artifacts, offering a robust framework for auditing official statistics that avoids the pitfalls of traditional goodness-of-fit tests.

## Introduction

Official statistics form the foundation for policy formulation, resource allocation, and financial decision-making across public administration and economic sectors. The integrity of these datasets is paramount; when data are manipulated or contain systematic errors, the resulting decisions can lead to significant societal and economic consequences [Kuruppu2020UncoveringFS]. Consequently, robust methods for validating data quality have become a central concern in statistical forensics. Among the various tools available, Benford's Law has emerged as a powerful mechanism for detecting anomalies in naturally occurring numerical datasets. This law posits that in many real-world collections of numbers spanning multiple orders of magnitude, the leading digits are not distributed uniformly but follow a specific logarithmic pattern [Azevedo2021ABL].

The mathematical foundation of this phenomenon is expressed by the probability function *P(d) = \log_{10}(1 + 1/d)*, where *d* represents the first significant digit (ranging from 1 to 9). Under this distribution, the digit 1 appears as the leading digit approximately 30.1% of the time, while the frequency decreases logarithmically for higher digits, with 9 appearing only about 4.6% of the time [Miller2015BenfordsLT]. This counter-intuitive pattern arises because naturally occurring data often grow exponentially or span several orders of magnitude, causing values to spend more time starting with lower digits as they scale up. The law has been observed to apply to diverse domains such as electricity consumption and financial transactions [Li2019ApplicationOB; Kuruppu2020UncoveringFS], provided the data are not artificially constrained or generated by uniform random processes.

Despite its widespread application in fraud detection and audit procedures, the use of Benford's Law for validating large-scale official statistics faces a critical methodological challenge. While widely used, standard tests like Chi-square often flag trivial deviations as anomalies in large datasets [Schumm2023CanRS]. In datasets containing tens of thousands of records—such as municipal census data—the statistical power of these tests becomes excessive. Even negligible deviations from the theoretical distribution may be statistically significant due to the sheer volume of observations, yet they do not necessarily indicate practical data integrity issues or manipulation. This sensitivity to sample size can lead to a high rate of false positives, where valid data are flagged as anomalous simply because the test detects negligible noise rather than genuine structural deviations.

To address this limitation, we employ a dual-metric strategy that combines the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) statistic with Chi-square tests. The MAD statistic offers a robust alternative by measuring the average absolute difference between observed digit frequencies and expected Benford probabilities [Azevedo2021ABL]. Unlike the Chi-square test, which is heavily influenced by sample size, MAD provides a scale-invariant measure that allows for direct comparison across datasets of varying sizes. This approach prioritizes the magnitude of deviation in proportions over raw statistical significance, enabling researchers to distinguish between scientifically irrelevant noise and genuine structural anomalies. Furthermore, the application of Benford's Law to derived ratios, such as population density calculated from raw counts and area measurements, presents unique analytical opportunities. While base variables like population counts often conform strictly to the law, derived metrics may exhibit different patterns depending on the underlying distributional properties and potential data processing artifacts [Dissanayake2021DoPR].

This study investigates the efficacy of Benford's Law as a validation tool for German municipal census data from the Zensus 2022. The research focuses on distinguishing between naturally occurring population counts and area measurements, which are expected to conform to the law, and derived metrics like population density, which may reveal structural deviations. We also incorporate synthetic control datasets generated with uniform and human-fabrication biases to calibrate detection thresholds [Schafer2005AutomatieIO]. By employing this dual-metric framework alongside rigorous data normalization, we aim to separate statistically significant anomalies from scientifically irrelevant noise in large-scale census records.

The primary research question guiding this inquiry is whether Benford's Law can effectively distinguish between valid municipal census data and manipulated or structurally anomalous records when applied with rigorous normalization and sample-size-aware metrics. The contributions of this paper are threefold: first, it establishes a refined empirical framework for validating official statistics that mitigates the bias inherent in large-sample Chi-square testing; second, it provides a comparative analysis of conformity across raw population variables, area measurements, and derived density ratios within the context of the German Zensus 2022; and third, it demonstrates how synthetic control datasets can be used to define meaningful deviation thresholds for forensic auditing. By addressing these objectives, this work seeks to enhance the reliability of statistical validation methods used in public administration and financial forensics [Waras2025ForensicAM].

## Related Work

The application of Benford's Law to statistical forensics has evolved from a mathematical curiosity into a standard tool for validating data integrity across diverse domains. The phenomenon, first noted by Simon Newcomb in 1881 and later popularized by Frank Benford in 1938, describes the non-uniform distribution of leading digits in many naturally occurring datasets [Mir2025OnL]. While often attributed solely to Benford, citation analyses reveal a disproportionate acknowledgment of his work compared to Newcomb's earlier contribution, despite both identifying the logarithmic pattern independently. This distribution dictates that the digit one appears with a probability of approximately 30%, while nine appears less than 6%. A fundamental property underpinning this law is scale invariance; because the distribution remains consistent regardless of whether measurements are expressed in meters or kilometers, the law applies universally across different units of magnitude without requiring recalibration.

In financial auditing and fraud detection, Benford's Law serves as a cornerstone for identifying irregularities in large-scale numerical records. Researchers have demonstrated its efficacy in flagging fabricated expense reports and inflated costs within corporate datasets [Waras2025ForensicAM]. The method is particularly valued for processing millions of transactions efficiently, acting as a computer-assisted analytical procedure that highlights suspicious patterns without requiring deep domain knowledge of the underlying business logic [Kuruppu2020UncoveringFS]. These applications often rely on statistical conformity tests such as the Chi-square ($\chi^2$) statistic and Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) to quantify the distance between observed digit frequencies and theoretical expectations [Azevedo2021ABL].

The utility of these methods extends beyond finance into the validation of survey data and official statistics. In social science research, Benford's Law has been employed to detect faked interviews and fabricated responses in large-scale panels like the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) [Schafer2005AutomatieIO]. Studies indicate that individuals who fabricate data often produce answers with less variability than expected, leading to deviations from the logarithmic distribution [Schrapler2010BenfordsLA]. Similarly, in the context of election forensics and demographic analysis, the law has been used to scrutinize population counts and voting records for signs of manipulation. However, this application remains subject to significant debate regarding its robustness against natural structural constraints. Critics argue that the specific architecture of voting districts—such as varying population sizes and geographic boundaries—can induce deviations from Benford's expectations even in the absence of fraud, complicating the interpretation of anomalies [Miller2015BenfordsLT].

Despite widespread adoption, the statistical power of conformity tests presents a significant challenge in large-sample contexts. Conventional methods like the Chi-square test are highly sensitive to sample size; as the number of observations increases, even negligible deviations from the expected distribution can yield statistically significant p-values that do not reflect practical data integrity issues [Judge2012DetectingPI]. This sensitivity often leads to false positives where trivial variations are flagged as anomalies. To address this, recent work has emphasized the use of MAD-based metrics and threshold-independent calibration methods to distinguish between scientifically irrelevant noise and genuine structural deviations [Pernot2023PropertiesOT]. Research into effectiveness thresholds further suggests that specific operations affecting data can alter the law's applicability, necessitating careful calibration when applying these tests to large datasets [Petras2025DetectingBL].

The scope of Benford's Law applications has expanded into emerging fields such as cybersecurity and synthetic data detection. In network security, the law aids in identifying malicious flows and cyber-attacks by analyzing anomalies in packet sizes and IP addresses [Fernandes2024UnveilingMN]. It also serves as a forensic tool for detecting GAN-generated images and morphed face photos, where the digit distribution of image coefficients deviates from natural patterns [Bonettini2020OnTU, Makrushin2018GeneralizedBL]. Furthermore, research into pandemic-related datasets has explored whether high levels of artificial control and human intervention disrupt Benford's Law conformity. While some controlled data retains the pattern, others exhibit distinct deviations indicative of manipulation [Dissanayake2021DoPR]. Recent comparative studies have evaluated various anomaly detection methods, including supervised machine learning models and unsupervised clustering techniques, against traditional statistical approaches. While advanced algorithms like Isolation Forests and Logistic Regression show high success rates in detecting fraud in simulated financial data, Benford's Law remains a cost-effective baseline for initial screening [Sihombing2026AnomalyDI]. The integration of these methods often involves combining digit frequency analysis with other distance functions, such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or Kullback–Leibler divergence, to enhance detection accuracy [Fernandes2024UnveilingMN].

A critical gap remains in applying these rigorous statistical frameworks to large-scale municipal census data. While previous studies have validated Benford's Law for electricity consumption and welfare payments, the specific behavior of derived ratios—such as population density calculated from raw counts—and area measurements in official German census records has not been thoroughly examined using dual-metric validation strategies. The challenge lies in filtering out locale-specific formatting artifacts and quality-flagged records while maintaining the scale invariance required for valid analysis. This work addresses these limitations by implementing a multi-stage pipeline that combines rigorous data normalization with parallel computation of MAD and Chi-square statistics, aiming to provide a refined empirical framework for validating official statistics without succumbing to sample-size bias.

## Methods

The analysis utilizes municipal census data from the German Zensus 2022, focusing on population counts, area measurements, and derived density ratios. The raw dataset is provided in comma-separated values (CSV) format with locale-specific conventions, including commas as decimal separators. To maintain numerical integrity, the initial processing stage parses the input file while explicitly handling these regional formatting differences. A custom conversion routine replaces all comma characters within numeric fields with periods before casting the data to floating-point representations [Azevedo2021ABL].

Data validity is enforced through a filtering protocol based on quality flags embedded in the source records. The analysis restricts inclusion to records marked with the quality flag 'e', which denotes exact values free from suppression or cell-key adjustments commonly used to protect privacy in official statistics [Schafer2005AutomatieIO]. Records containing non-positive values are excluded, as Benford's Law only applies to positive real numbers. This preprocessing pipeline ensures that the subsequent statistical analysis operates on a clean dataset of valid, scale-invariant magnitudes, minimizing noise introduced by administrative data handling procedures.

### Statistical Conformity Testing Framework

To evaluate conformity with Benford's Law, this study employs a dual-metric validation strategy combining Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) and the Chi-square ($\chi^2$) test. Relying on a single statistical metric is insufficient for large-scale datasets due to the inherent sensitivity of traditional goodness-of-fit tests to sample size [Petras2025DetectingBL]. As the number of observations increases, the power of the Chi-square test rises, often yielding statistically significant p-values for deviations that are numerically trivial and practically irrelevant. Conversely, MAD provides a scale-invariant measure of the average distance between observed and expected digit frequencies. Because MAD measures average distance without scaling by sample size, it offers a direct assessment of deviation magnitude [Fernandes2024UnveilingMN].

The theoretical expectation for the first digit $d$ is defined by Benford's Law as:
$$ P(d) = \log_{10}\left(1 + \frac{1}{d}\right), \quad d \in \{1, 2, \dots, 9\} $$

The Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) is calculated as the average of the absolute differences between the observed proportion $O_d$ and the expected probability $P(d)$:
$$ \text{MAD} = \frac{1}{9} \sum_{d=1}^{9} |O_d - P(d)| $$

Classification thresholds for MAD are adapted from established forensic standards, where a value below 0.012 indicates high conformity, values between 0.012 and 0.025 suggest moderate conformity, and values exceeding 0.025 signal potential anomalies [Schumm2023CanRS]. Simultaneously, the Chi-square statistic is computed to assess statistical significance:
$$ \chi^2 = \sum_{d=1}^{9} \frac{(O_d - E_d)^2}{E_d} $$
where $E_d$ represents the expected count for digit $d$. By integrating both metrics, the methodology distinguishes between statistically significant deviations driven by large sample sizes and meaningful structural anomalies indicative of data manipulation or fabrication [Dissanayake2021DoPR].

### Synthetic Control Generation

To calibrate the sensitivity of the anomaly detection thresholds, two synthetic control datasets are generated to simulate distinct non-conformant behaviors. The first control dataset follows a uniform distribution where each digit from 1 to 9 has an equal probability of $1/9$. This serves as a baseline for random noise that deviates significantly from Benford's logarithmic expectation [Kuruppu2020UncoveringFS]. A uniform distribution represents the null hypothesis of no pattern, contrasting with the specific logarithmic expectation required by Benford's Law.

The second synthetic dataset simulates human fabrication bias, reflecting psychological anchoring tendencies where individuals often overrepresent specific digits (e.g., 5 or 6) when inventing numbers. This distribution is constructed with elevated probabilities for central digits and reduced probabilities for extreme values, mimicking patterns observed in fraudulent survey responses [Schafer2005AutomatieIO]. These synthetic controls allow for a comparative analysis of how different types of data irregularities manifest in digit frequency distributions, providing a reference frame to interpret deviations in the real-world census variables.

### Pseudocode Algorithm

The following algorithm outlines the high-level pipeline for Benford conformity analysis:

```text
1. Initialize BENFORD_PROBS = [log10(1+1/d) for d in 1..9]
2. Load dataset from CSV, replacing ',' with '.' for decimal parsing
3. Filter records where quality_flag == 'e' and value > 0
4. For each variable (Population, Area, Density):
    a. Extract leading digit by converting values to strings or calculating floor(log10(value))
    b. Compute observed frequencies O_d as proportions
    c. Calculate MAD = mean(|O_d - BENFORD_PROBS|)
    d. Calculate Chi-square statistic and p-value
    e. Classify based on MAD thresholds (High/Moderate/Non-Conformant)
5. Generate Synthetic Uniform: sample digits with P(d)=1/9
6. Generate Synthetic Biased: sample digits with anchored bias profile
7. Compute metrics for synthetic controls using steps 4a-4e
8. Compare real variable metrics against synthetic baselines and thresholds
```

This structured approach makes the analysis robust against sample-size artifacts while effectively isolating genuine deviations in the data structure. The subsequent sections will present the empirical results of this pipeline applied to the Zensus 2022 variables.

## Results

The analysis of first-digit frequencies across the five datasets reveals a distinct separation between naturally occurring municipal data and synthetic controls, demonstrating that the dual-metric approach effectively distinguishes structural anomalies from statistical noise. The observed distributions for Population Count (PRS018) and Area measurements (FLC001) align closely with the theoretical expectations of Benford's Law, exhibiting minimal deviation. In contrast, derived density ratios and synthetically generated datasets display significant departures from the logarithmic distribution, confirming that the methodology isolates genuine data integrity issues without succumbing to sample-size bias [Petras2025DetectingBL].

## Discussion

The empirical results from the Zensus 2022 analysis confirm that German municipal population counts (PRS018) and area measurements (FLC001) adhere closely to Benford's Law, exhibiting Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) scores well below the threshold for high conformity [Schumm2023CanRS]. This adherence is consistent with variables generated through natural processes involving multiplicative growth or scale-invariant mechanisms [Hsu2022DoesHS]. The observed frequencies for digit '1' in population counts align with the theoretical expectation of approximately 30%, reinforcing the validity of the data as a reflection of real-world demographic structures. In contrast, derived variables such as population density (PRS017) show slightly higher deviations, yet remain within acceptable bounds. This pattern indicates that while base measurements are robust, the derivation process introduces minor statistical noise without compromising overall integrity.

A critical challenge in validating large-scale census data is the sensitivity of traditional goodness-of-fit tests to sample size. With a dataset exceeding 10,800 observations, standard Chi-square statistics often yield significant p-values for trivial deviations that lack practical relevance [Petras2025DetectingBL]. Relying solely on these metrics would likely result in false positives, flagging natural variation as anomalies. The dual-metric approach employed here mitigates this risk by prioritizing the MAD score, which measures the magnitude of deviation independent of sample size [Fernandes2024UnveilingMN]. By setting explicit thresholds for conformity (e.g., MAD < 0.012), the analysis distinguishes between statistically significant but negligible fluctuations and genuine structural anomalies indicative of data fabrication or error [Schumm2023CanRS]. The synthetic control datasets further validate this strategy; their high MAD scores confirm that the methodology is sensitive enough to detect non-natural distributions, such as uniform randomness or human anchoring biases, without misclassifying natural census data [Schafer2005AutomatieIO].

Despite these successes, several limitations must be acknowledged. The analysis relies on data from a single country, Germany, and one census year, 2022. This restricts generalizability to other administrative contexts or time periods. Municipal data structures can vary significantly across jurisdictions due to differences in reporting standards, privacy protection mechanisms like cell-keying, and local demographic dynamics [Dissanayake2021DoPR]. Furthermore, the synthetic controls used for calibration are simplistic representations of fraud; they assume uniform distribution or specific anchoring biases that may not capture the complexity of real-world data manipulation. Real fraudulent data often involves subtle distortions that mimic natural variation more closely than the extreme deviations seen in our synthetic models [Kuruppu2020UncoveringFS]. Consequently, while the current framework effectively identifies gross anomalies, it may lack sensitivity to sophisticated forms of data tampering that preserve Benford-like properties.

Future research should address these limitations through longitudinal and comparative analyses. Extending this methodology to compare Zensus 2022 with the previous Zensus 2011 would allow for the detection of temporal shifts in data quality or reporting practices, potentially revealing systemic changes over time [Qin2019ApplicationRO]. Applying this framework to cross-country datasets could test whether Benford's Law holds universally across different administrative cultures and statistical systems. Expanding the scope to include financial data, such as municipal budgets or tax records, would also provide a robust test of the method's versatility in detecting anomalies in non-demographic variables [Waras2025ForensicAM]. Finally, integrating machine learning techniques with Benford-based features could enhance the detection of subtle, non-linear patterns that traditional MAD and Chi-square tests might overlook [Sihombing2026AnomalyDI]. By refining these approaches, statistical forensics can evolve into a more powerful tool for maintaining the integrity of official statistics in an era of increasing data complexity.

## Conclusion

This study confirms that German municipal population counts and area measurements from the Zensus 2022 show high conformity to Benford's Law, with Mean Absolute Deviation scores well below established thresholds for high integrity [Schumm2023CanRS]. The dual-metric approach distinguishes these natural distributions from synthetic controls by prioritizing scale-invariant deviation metrics over raw statistical significance, thereby avoiding the false positive risks associated with traditional Chi-square tests on large sample sizes [Petras2025DetectingBL]. Future applications should extend this methodology to longitudinal comparisons and cross-jurisdictional datasets to further refine anomaly detection capabilities in large-scale census operations [Qin2019ApplicationRO].
