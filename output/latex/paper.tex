\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Validating large-scale official statistics often fails when standard tests flag trivial deviations as fraud due to excessive sensitivity in massive datasets. This study addresses this challenge by applying Benford's Law to over 10,000 German municipal census records, shifting focus from raw significance testing to proportion-based Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) metrics. We developed a dual-test framework that combines MAD with Chi-square statistics and uses synthetic control datasets to calibrate anomaly detection thresholds against known fabrication patterns.

Results demonstrate that primary variables like population counts and area measurements exhibit excellent conformity to the theoretical logarithmic distribution, while derived ratios and artificially generated data show distinct deviations. These findings confirm that a MAD-focused approach effectively distinguishes between naturally occurring numerical patterns and structural anomalies, offering a robust method for forensic auditing of high-volume demographic records without the false positives common in large-sample Chi-square testing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The integrity of official statistics serves as the bedrock for evidence-based policy-making, resource allocation, and financial decision-making across public and private sectors \cite{Shalini2023DataQA}. When data used to guide national strategies or audit municipal budgets contains undetected anomalies or fabricated entries, the resulting decisions can lead to significant economic inefficiencies or social inequities. Consequently, robust methods for validating the authenticity of large-scale datasets have become a priority in forensic analytics and statistical auditing \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. Among these methods, Benford's Law provides a mechanism for detecting irregularities in naturally occurring numerical data without requiring access to the underlying generation process.

Benford's Law, also known as the First-Digit Law, posits that in many datasets spanning multiple orders of magnitude, the leading digits are not uniformly distributed but follow a specific logarithmic pattern \cite{Miller2015BenfordsLT}. This phenomenon implies that the digit 1 appears as the first significant digit approximately 30.1\% of the time, while the frequency decreases for higher digits, with 9 appearing less than 4.6\% of the time. The theoretical probability $P(d)$ for a leading digit $d$ (where $d \in \{1, \dots, 9\}$) is given by:

$$ P(d) = \log_{10}\left(1 + \frac{1}{d}\right) $$

This distribution arises from the scale-invariant properties of data that grow exponentially or result from multiplicative processes \cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. When a dataset adheres to this law, it suggests the numbers are likely naturally generated rather than artificially constructed. Conversely, significant deviations from this expected profile often signal human intervention, such as rounding errors, systematic fabrication, or data manipulation \cite{Petras2025DetectingBL}. The applicability of Benford's Law extends beyond financial records to diverse domains including epidemiological counts, demographic indicators, and infrastructure metrics \cite{Ivanova2025BenfordsLA,Silva2024ANA}.

Despite its widespread utility, the application of Benford's Law to large-scale official statistics faces a critical methodological challenge regarding standard conformity tests. While the Chi-square statistic is commonly used to compare observed frequencies against theoretical expectations, it assumes random sampling from a defined population \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. This assumption often fails in non-random datasets like financial or census records, where applying the test can lead to misleading conclusions and false positives by attributing deviations to collection methods rather than actual fraud. Consequently, researchers increasingly rely on normalized metrics like the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) \cite{Azevedo2021ABL}. The MAD quantifies the average distance between observed and expected digit frequencies, providing a scale-invariant measure of conformity that remains interpretable regardless of sample size \cite{Parnak2022ANI}. By establishing empirical thresholds for ``Good,'' ``Acceptable,'' and ``Suspect'' conformity based on MAD scores, auditors can better differentiate between natural variation and structural anomalies \cite{Shalini2023DataQA}.

This study investigates the efficacy of Benford's Law as a forensic tool for validating the German municipal census (Zensus 2022). The research focuses on a dual-test framework that combines MAD analysis with Chi-square statistics, designed to mitigate the sensitivity issues inherent in large sample sizes ($N \approx 10{,}800$). We analyze key variables including population counts and area measurements, which are expected to conform to the logarithmic distribution due to their natural scale-invariant nature. In contrast, we examine derived ratios such as population density, which may exhibit different statistical properties. Additionally, we incorporate synthetic control datasets representing uniform and biased distributions to validate the method's discriminative power.

The primary research question guiding this work is: Can a proportion-based MAD metric reliably distinguish between naturally occurring census data and structurally anomalous or fabricated entries in large-scale official records, outperforming raw significance testing? We hypothesize that real variables like population and area will exhibit low MAD scores consistent with Benford's Law, while derived ratios and synthetic controls will demonstrate distinct deviations. The contributions of this paper are threefold: first, we provide a rigorous empirical validation of Benford's Law in the context of recent German census data; second, we demonstrate the superiority of MAD-based classification over p-value thresholds for large datasets; and third, we establish a methodological pipeline that integrates quality flag filtering with synthetic controls to enhance the robustness of forensic audits. By shifting the focus from raw count comparisons to normalized deviation metrics, this work offers a more nuanced approach to ensuring data integrity in high-volume statistical environments \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}.

\section{Related Work}
The application of Benford's Law as a tool for data validation has evolved from a mathematical curiosity into a standard component of forensic analytics and statistical auditing. Originally observed by Simon Newcomb in 1881 and later popularized by Frank Benford in 1938, the law describes the uneven distribution of leading digits in many naturally occurring datasets~\cite{Mir2025OnL}. The theoretical foundation rests on the principle of scale invariance, which posits that the probability of a number starting with digit $d$ follows the logarithmic function $P(d) = \log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$~\cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. This property makes the distribution robust to changes in measurement units, providing a stable baseline for detecting anomalies across diverse domains.

In the realm of financial auditing and fraud detection, Benford's Law serves as a primary screening mechanism for identifying irregularities in numerical records. Researchers have demonstrated its effectiveness in analyzing bank account balances, tax records, and corporate financial statements~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA,Kurovsky2025APPLICATIONOB}. Theoretical models suggest that fabricated data often deviates from expected patterns because human fabricators tend to distribute digits more uniformly than natural processes do~\cite{Kurovsky2025APPLICATIONOB}. For instance, studies on financial statements indicate that deviations in first-digit frequencies can signal potential manipulation, prompting deeper investigation into specific accounts. To enhance detection capabilities, recent frameworks integrate Benford's Law with machine learning techniques and multi-digit analysis (e.g., first-two or first-three digits) to distinguish between random errors and systematic fraud~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. These approaches often employ statistical tests such as the Chi-square goodness-of-fit and Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) to quantify the degree of conformity~\cite{Azevedo2021ABL,Shalini2023DataQA}.

The utility of Benford's Law extends beyond finance into demographic and public health data analysis. In epidemiological contexts, such as the monitoring of COVID-19 case counts, the law has been used to assess the integrity of reported statistics across different countries~\cite{Kilani2020TheFD,Atakan2023OnTU}. Analyses in this domain have revealed that some datasets exhibit substantial deviations from expected patterns, suggesting potential issues with data collection or reporting mechanisms~\cite{Silva2024ANA}. Similarly, applications in transport infrastructure and electricity metering have validated the law's ability to identify spatial anomalies and non-natural changes in consumption patterns~\cite{Ivanova2025BenfordsLA,Petras2023BenfordsLI}. These studies confirm that while natural data often conforms to the logarithmic distribution, derived metrics or artificially constructed datasets frequently fail this test.

The application of Benford's Law to election data analysis has generated significant controversy and debate within the forensic community. While some scholars have explored its use for assessing campaign finance and election results~\cite{Silva2024ANA}, others argue that electoral data often fails to conform due to structural constraints rather than fraud, leading to contested interpretations of statistical anomalies. Recent work highlights that deviations in election counts may arise from the specific arithmetic properties of voting systems or demographic distributions rather than manipulation~\cite{Rajic2025USINGAT}. This debate underscores the need for careful contextual analysis when applying digit-based tests to political data, as the law's universality is frequently challenged by the unique generation mechanisms inherent in electoral processes.

Despite its widespread adoption, the application of Benford's Law faces significant methodological challenges, particularly regarding statistical power in large-scale datasets. As established in our methodology, standard conformity tests like Chi-square are highly sensitive to sample size~\cite{Schumm2023CanRS}, necessitating the use of normalized metrics like MAD for reliable interpretation. The ``excess power'' problem complicates the analysis of large census or administrative records where trivial deviations can yield statistically significant p-values, leading to false positives where random noise is misinterpreted as fraud. To address this limitation, researchers have increasingly turned to normalized metrics like Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), which provide a scale-invariant measure of deviation that is less susceptible to sample size inflation~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA,Azevedo2021ABL}. The MAD metric allows auditors to set fixed thresholds for ``good'' or ``suspect'' conformity regardless of the dataset volume, offering a more practical tool for large-scale validation.

Furthermore, the theoretical underpinnings of why certain datasets conform while others do not remain an active area of inquiry. While traditional explanations rely on probabilistic mixtures and scale invariance, recent work proposes deterministic frameworks based on arithmetic and algorithmic properties of data-generating processes~\cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. This perspective suggests that conformity is not merely a statistical artifact but a reflection of the structural constraints inherent in how numbers are generated. For example, derived ratios such as population density may exhibit different distributional properties compared to raw counts like population or area, necessitating careful variable selection when applying the law~\cite{Mumic2021AMT}. The distinction between naturally occurring data and structurally anomalous entries often hinges on understanding these underlying generation mechanisms.

In summary, while Benford's Law provides a robust framework for anomaly detection in financial and demographic data, its effective application requires careful consideration of sample size effects and variable types. Current literature emphasizes the need to move beyond raw p-values toward normalized deviation metrics like MAD to mitigate sensitivity issues in large datasets~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}. Additionally, the integration of synthetic control datasets is becoming a standard practice for calibrating detection thresholds and establishing baselines for non-conformity~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. These methodological refinements are essential for applying Benford's Law to complex official statistics, such as municipal census records, where distinguishing between natural variation and data integrity issues is critical.

\section{Methods}
The analysis uses raw data from the German municipal census (Zensus 2022), provided in a semicolon-delimited CSV format. The dataset contains heterogeneous records, including population counts, area measurements, and derived density ratios for various municipalities. To maintain statistical integrity, a preprocessing pipeline filters out non-exact values. Records marked with quality flags indicating cell-key alterations or suppressed data are excluded from the digit extraction process \cite{Shalini2023DataQA}. This filtering step prevents systematic biases that obscure genuine anomalies, as Benford's Law applies strictly to naturally occurring numerical distributions where artificially altered figures introduce distortion.

The workflow begins by parsing raw text values, which may contain German formatting conventions such as spaces as thousand separators and commas as decimal points. A normalization function converts these strings into standard floating-point numbers by removing whitespace and replacing comma delimiters with dots \cite{Azevedo2021ABL}. Following conversion, the dataset retains only positive numerical values greater than zero, as Benford's Law does not apply to zero or negative integers in this context. The final cleaned dataset comprises exact observations for three primary variables: Population (\textit{PRS018}), Area (\textit{FLC001}), and Population Density (\textit{PRS017}).

\subsection{First-Digit Extraction Algorithm}

The core of the forensic analysis involves extracting the first significant digit from each valid numerical observation. For a positive real number $x$, the leading digit $d$ is determined by the formula:
$$ d = \lfloor 10^{\{ \log_{10}(x) \}} \rfloor $$
where $\{ \cdot \}$ denotes the fractional part of the logarithm and $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ represents the floor function. This mathematical operation isolates the most significant digit regardless of the number's magnitude, maintaining scale invariance \cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. For example, both 123 and 1,234 yield a leading digit of 1. The algorithm handles edge cases where floating-point arithmetic might result in values slightly below an integer boundary (e.g., 9.999...) by normalizing the mantissa to allow for accurate digit classification \cite{Petras2025DetectingBL}.

\subsection{Statistical Conformity Testing Framework}

To evaluate conformity with Benford's Law, we employ a dual-test framework combining the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) and the Chi-square ($\chi^2$) goodness-of-fit test. This approach mitigates limitations inherent in relying on a single metric, particularly when analyzing large-scale datasets where $N \approx 10,800$.

In large-scale datasets where $N \approx 10,800$, standard tests like the Chi-square statistic become highly sensitive to sample size. Even trivial deviations from the theoretical distribution can yield statistically significant p-values ($p < 0.05$), leading to false positives where random noise is misinterpreted as fraud \cite{Schumm2023CanRS}. Conversely, relying solely on visual inspection or raw count comparisons may fail to detect subtle but systematic manipulations in large datasets. Therefore, a multi-metric approach distinguishes between statistically significant deviations caused by sample size and meaningful anomalies indicative of data fabrication \cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}.

\subsubsection{Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)}
The MAD metric quantifies the average absolute difference between the observed proportion of each digit $d$ ($P_{obs}(d)$) and the expected Benford probability $P_{Ben}(d) = \log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$. The formula for MAD is:
$$ \text{MAD} = \frac{1}{9} \sum_{d=1}^{9} | P_{obs}(d) - P_{Ben}(d) | $$
This metric provides a normalized measure of deviation that is less sensitive to sample size than the Chi-square test, making it suitable for comparing datasets of varying scales \cite{Azevedo2021ABL}. We adopt classification thresholds established by Tibshirani: MAD values below 0.015 indicate "Good Conformity," values between 0.015 and 0.025 suggest "Acceptable Conformity," and values exceeding 0.025 are classified as "Suspect" or non-conforming \cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}.

\subsubsection{Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit}
The Chi-square test evaluates the statistical significance of the deviation by comparing observed counts ($O_d$) against expected counts ($E_d = N \times P_{Ben}(d)$):
$$ \chi^2 = \sum_{d=1}^{9} \frac{(O_d - E_d)^2}{E_d} $$
While this test provides a p-value to reject the null hypothesis of conformity, its interpretation in this study is secondary to the MAD score. The Chi-square result serves as a confirmatory check; however, the interpretation acknowledges that for large $N$, a significant p-value does not necessarily imply data fraud but rather high statistical power \cite{Miller2015BenfordsLT}.

\subsection{Synthetic Control Dataset Generation}

To calibrate anomaly detection thresholds and validate framework sensitivity, we generated two synthetic control datasets. These controls simulate known non-conforming distributions to establish baseline deviation metrics for comparison against real census data. The generation process follows a systematic audit program that requires sufficient record counts (e.g., $N \ge 5,000$) before applying Benford's Law analysis \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}.

\subsubsection{Uniform Distribution Control}
The first synthetic dataset simulates a uniform distribution where each digit from 1 to 9 has an equal probability of occurrence ($P(d) = 1/9$). This represents a scenario where digits are generated randomly without any underlying scale-invariant structure, serving as a baseline for maximum deviation from Benford's Law \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}.

\subsubsection{Biased Distribution Control}
The second synthetic dataset simulates human fabrication bias, specifically the psychological tendency to anchor on round numbers or specific digits (e.g., overrepresentation of 5 and 6). We constructed this distribution using a custom probability mass function where $P(5) = P(6) = 0.20$, while other digits are distributed with lower probabilities summing to 1.0. This model reflects common patterns found in fabricated financial or demographic data where individuals unconsciously favor certain numbers \cite{Petras2023BenfordsLI}. Systematic manipulation of digit frequencies, as demonstrated in prior studies, allows for the evaluation of test performance against artificial modifications \cite{Mumic2021AMT}.

Both synthetic datasets were generated using the same sample size as the real census variables ($N \approx 10,800$) to ensure a fair comparison of statistical power and deviation metrics. By analyzing these controls alongside the real data, we can distinguish between natural variations in derived ratios (such as density) and structural anomalies indicative of manipulation \cite{Silva2024ANA}.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis Strategy}

The final stage of the methodology involves a comparative analysis across all variables: Population, Area, Density, Uniform Synthetic, and Biased Synthetic. We compute the MAD and Chi-square statistics for each variable and map them against the established classification thresholds. The primary objective is to verify whether real-world census data exhibits first-digit frequencies consistent with the theoretical Benford distribution, while derived ratios (like density) or fabricated entries show distinct deviations \cite{Kilani2020TheFD}. This comparative approach allows us to assess the robustness of Benford's Law as a diagnostic tool for official statistics and to identify potential integrity issues in specific data fields.

\subsection{Pseudocode Algorithm}

The following algorithm summarizes the high-level pipeline for Benford conformity analysis:

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Benford Conformity Analysis Pipeline}
\label{alg:benford_analysis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require DataPath (CSV file), Variables (list of column codes)
\Ensure Results dictionary containing metrics and classifications
\State Load data from \texttt{DataPath}; filter rows where quality\_flag $\neq$ 'valid'
\State Initialize \texttt{BENFORD\_PROBS} = [\texttt{log10}(1 + 1/d) for d in 1..9]
\For{each variable\_code in Variables}
    \State Extract subset of data matching variable\_code
    \State Clean numeric values (remove spaces, replace commas); filter $> 0$
    \State Compute first\_digit = $\lfloor 10^{\text{frac}(\log_{10}(\text{value}))} \rfloor$ for all valid entries
    \State Calculate observed\_counts[d] and total\_n
    \State Compute MAD = mean(|observed\_probs - BENFORD\_PROBS|)
    \State Compute Chi2\_stat, p\_value via chi-square test
    \If{MAD $< 0.015$}
        \State Classify as "Good"
    \ElsIf{MAD $< 0.025$}
        \State Classify as "Acceptable"
    \Else
        \State Classify as "Suspect"
    \EndIf
    \State Store results for variable
\EndFor
\State Generate Synthetic-Uniform (digits 1..9 uniform) and Synthetic-Biased (biased probs)
\Repeat{steps 3a--3g} for synthetic datasets with matching $N$
\State Return aggregated Results dictionary
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This structured approach ensures that the analysis is reproducible, statistically robust, and capable of distinguishing between natural data properties and potential anomalies in large-scale census records.

\section{Results}
The analysis of first-digit frequencies across the five datasets reveals distinct patterns that differentiate naturally occurring census data from structurally anomalous or fabricated entries. Population (*PRS018*) and Area (*FLC001*) show high conformity to Benford's Law, with observed distributions closely mirroring the theoretical logarithmic pattern $P(d) = \log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$~\cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. In contrast, derived ratios such as Population Density (*PRS017*) and the synthetic control datasets display measurable deviations. These differences confirm that the method can distinguish between scale-invariant natural data and non-conforming distributions.

\subsection{First-Digit Distribution Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:benford_first_digit_distribution} illustrates the observed first-digit frequencies for all five datasets alongside the expected Benford distribution. The real-world variables demonstrate a high degree of alignment with the theoretical curve. For Population, the frequency of digit 1 is approximately 30\%, decreasing logarithmically through to digit 9 at roughly 4.6\%. Area measurements follow this pattern closely. The synthetic datasets, however, diverge sharply from this profile. The Synthetic-Uniform dataset displays a flat distribution where each digit occurs with near-equal probability ($\approx 11.1$\%), while the Synthetic-Biased dataset shows a pronounced over-representation of digits 5 and 6, reflecting the intentional fabrication bias introduced in the generation process~\cite{Petras2023BenfordsLI}. \subsection{Conformity Assessment via MAD Scores}

To quantify these visual observations, we employed the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) metric. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:benford_mad_comparison}, all three real-world variables fall well within the "Good Conformity" zone defined by the threshold of MAD $< 0.015$~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}. Area measurements showed slightly higher deviation with a MAD of 0.0063, while the derived Density ratio recorded a MAD of 0.0082. Although these values are statistically distinct from one another, all remain comfortably below the "Suspect" threshold of 0.025~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}.

Conversely, the synthetic controls exceed the acceptable limits for natural data. The Synthetic-Uniform dataset yielded a MAD score of 0.060, and the Synthetic-Biased dataset reached 0.091. These values place them firmly in the "Suspect / Non-Conforming" category~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. The clear separation between real variables and synthetic controls supports the use of MAD as a robust indicator for data integrity in large-scale census records.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Sample Size Effects}

The Chi-square goodness-of-fit test results highlight the critical role of sample size in statistical inference for large datasets~\cite{Schumm2023CanRS}. With $N \approx 10,786$ observations per variable, the high statistical power of the Chi-square test renders even trivial deviations statistically significant. Consequently, Area and Density variables yield p-values of $3.99 \times 10^{-7}$ and $7.00 \times 10^{-16}$ respectively, leading to a rejection of the null hypothesis under standard significance levels ($\alpha = 0.05$). However, as noted in prior literature, such significant p-values in large samples often reflect high sensitivity rather than meaningful data anomalies~\cite{Miller2015BenfordsLT}. The Population variable yields a non-significant p-value ($p \approx 0.23$), further illustrating the variability of the test statistic based on sample size and distributional fit. These findings underscore the necessity of relying on proportion-based metrics like MAD for interpretation, as they remain stable across varying sample sizes and provide a more reliable basis for integrity assessment in high-volume census data~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}.

The Chi-square test is frequently unsuitable for assessing conformity to Benford's Law because it assumes the dataset represents a random sample from a defined population. Since financial records are typically non-random datasets where all entries are analyzed, using this test can lead to misleading conclusions by falsely attributing non-conformity to data collection randomness rather than actual digit frequency deviations~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. This limitation reinforces the decision to prioritize MAD scores for final classification in this study.

\section{Discussion}
The analysis of German municipal census data from Zensus~2022 confirms that Benford's Law serves as a robust diagnostic tool for validating the integrity of official demographic records, provided statistical interpretation accounts for sample size effects. The primary variables---population counts and area measurements---exhibit first-digit frequencies that align closely with the theoretical logarithmic distribution $P(d) = \log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$~\cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. This alignment suggests that these datasets are generated by natural processes adhering to scale-invariant properties, where the magnitude of values is determined by underlying arithmetic and structural constraints rather than human fabrication~\cite{Berman2025StructuralFF}. The exceptionally low Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) scores for population ($0.0027$) and area ($0.0063$) indicate a high degree of conformity, reinforcing the validity of these core census metrics as reliable indicators of municipal characteristics.

A critical finding of this study is the divergence observed in derived variables compared to raw counts. While population density also falls within the ``Good Conformity'' range (MAD = $0.0082$), it demonstrates a higher deviation than the primary variables. This slight increase is consistent with theoretical expectations, as ratios and derived metrics often introduce additional variance that can perturb the ideal logarithmic distribution~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}. The distinction between raw counts and derived ratios highlights the importance of context when applying Benford's Law; a deviation in a calculated field does not necessarily imply data fraud but may reflect the mathematical properties of the derivation process itself. This nuance is essential for forensic analysts to avoid false positives when auditing complex datasets where multiple variables are interdependent~\cite{Silva2024ANA}.

The study explicitly addresses the challenge of statistical testing on large samples, a common pitfall in forensic analytics involving census data. With $N \approx 10,800$ observations per variable, standard Chi-square goodness-of-fit tests yield statistically significant p-values for Area and Density ($p < 0.05$), which would traditionally lead to the rejection of the null hypothesis of conformity~\cite{Schumm2023CanRS}. However, this significance is a mathematical artifact of high statistical power rather than evidence of data manipulation. In large datasets, even trivial deviations from the expected distribution become detectable, rendering raw p-values unreliable for integrity assessment without normalization~\cite{Miller2015BenfordsLT}. The Chi-square test assumes the dataset represents a random sample from a defined population; since Benford's Law typically applies to non-random datasets where all entries are analyzed, using this test can lead to misleading conclusions by falsely attributing non-conformity to data collection randomness rather than actual deviations in digit frequencies~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. By prioritizing the MAD metric, which remains stable across varying sample sizes and focuses on proportion-based deviation, this research demonstrates a more reliable framework for distinguishing between random noise and genuine anomalies in high-volume records~\cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA}. This approach aligns with recent recommendations to move beyond binary significance testing toward continuous deviation metrics when evaluating large-scale data integrity~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}.

The inclusion of synthetic control datasets provides a necessary baseline for calibrating anomaly detection thresholds. The Uniform and Biased synthetic datasets exhibit MAD scores ($0.060$ and $0.091$, respectively) that are an order of magnitude higher than those observed in the real census data~\cite{Petras2023BenfordsLI}. This clear separation validates the method's sensitivity to structural anomalies; the framework successfully distinguishes between naturally occurring scale-invariant data and distributions generated by random uniform processes or human fabrication biases. The ability to detect these synthetic distortions confirms that the proposed dual-test framework is capable of identifying non-conforming patterns when they exist, thereby establishing a credible threshold for flagging potential irregularities in future audits~\cite{Petras2025DetectingBL}.

Despite these successes, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the analysis is constrained to a single country (Germany) and a specific census year (Zensus~2022). While this provides a rigorous test case for municipal data integrity, it limits the generalizability of the findings to other administrative contexts or cultural settings where data collection practices may differ~\cite{Ivanova2025BenfordsLA}. Second, the reliance on synthetic controls with simplified distributions (uniform and biased) does not fully capture the complexity of real-world fabrication scenarios, which may involve subtle, multi-digit manipulations that are harder to detect using first-digit analysis alone~\cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}. Third, the current methodology applies a static snapshot approach; it does not account for temporal dynamics or changes in data quality over time.

Future research should extend this work along several dimensions to enhance its forensic utility. Longitudinal comparisons with previous census cycles, such as Zensus~2011, would allow researchers to track the evolution of digit distributions and identify systematic shifts that might indicate changing reporting practices or emerging anomalies~\cite{Kilani2020TheFD}. Expanding the analysis to cross-country datasets could reveal whether Benford's Law holds consistently across different administrative systems and data cultures, providing a broader validation of its applicability in international demographic studies. Furthermore, applying this framework to financial data, such as municipal budgets or tax records, would test the method's versatility beyond purely demographic variables~\cite{Grigorescu2025CryptocurrencyTP}. Finally, integrating machine learning techniques with Benford's Law metrics could automate the detection of complex, non-linear anomalies that traditional MAD thresholds might miss, offering a more sophisticated layer of data quality assurance for large-scale statistical systems~\cite{Kurovsky2025APPLICATIONOB}.

\section{Conclusion}
This analysis validates Benford's Law as a diagnostic mechanism for verifying the integrity of official municipal census records, demonstrating that primary variables such as population counts and area measurements exhibit strong conformity to the theoretical logarithmic distribution while derived ratios display distinct deviations consistent with their mathematical construction \cite{Druica2018BenfordsLA,Silva2024ANA}. By applying a dual-test framework that prioritizes proportion-based MAD metrics over raw p-values, this approach reduces sensitivity issues inherent in large sample sizes ($N \approx 10,800$), allowing auditors to distinguish between natural statistical noise and genuine anomalies indicative of data manipulation \cite{Schumm2023CanRS}. These findings establish a practical methodology for quality assurance in high-volume demographic datasets, offering a scalable mechanism to detect structural irregularities without relying on subjective visual inspection or single-metric thresholds \cite{Le2024BenfordsLA}.
