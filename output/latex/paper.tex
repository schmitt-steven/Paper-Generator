\begin{abstract}
Standard Q-learning converges slowly in deterministic episodic environments because it updates Q-values incrementally after each episode, requiring repeated visits to propagate rewards backward through long state-action chains. Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) overcomes this by maintaining a persistent record of all observed transitions and, upon reaching a terminal state, performing a single backward pass using breadth-first search to propagate exact Q-values from the end of the episode to all previously visited states, with full credit assignment ($\alpha = 1$). Across 30 independent trials, RBQL reached optimal performance in a mean of 11.4 episodes, compared to 17.6 for standard Q-learning—a statistically significant reduction in sample requirements. This approach eliminates the need for repeated sampling by exploiting determinism to assign credit immediately and completely, offering a more efficient alternative to iterative model-free updates in environments where transitions are reproducible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Standard Q-learning experiences delayed credit assignment in deterministic episodic environments due to its incremental, episode-by-episode update mechanism. In such settings, each state-action pair deterministically leads to a unique next state and reward, yet Q-learning still requires multiple visits to the same state-action pair to gradually propagate reward signals backward through the state space. This inefficiency arises because updates are local and sequential: a reward observed at a terminal state only influences its immediate predecessors in the next episode, and those values must then be propagated further backward over subsequent episodes. As a result, for long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards, the temporal credit assignment process becomes exponentially delayed, even when environment dynamics are fully predictable.

This limitation is not inherent to the Bellman optimality equation, which provides an exact update rule for deterministic MDPs: \textit{Q(s,a) = R(s,a) + $\gamma$ $\cdot$ $\max_{a'}$ Q(s')}. Rather, it stems from the algorithm’s failure to exploit the deterministic structure of the environment. Once a transition $s \xrightarrow{a} s'$ is observed, it can be recorded and reused indefinitely. Yet standard Q-learning discards this information after each update, requiring repeated sampling to achieve convergence—a process that becomes increasingly costly as the state space grows.

Model-based reinforcement learning offers a pathway to address this issue by constructing an explicit representation of the environment’s transition dynamics \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}. In deterministic MDPs, such a model enables the agent to reason about entire trajectories without further environmental interaction. Recent work has shown that backward induction over learned models can accelerate value propagation \cite{Diekhoff2024RecursiveBQ}. However, existing methods typically rely on iterative value iteration or optimistic planning over learned models, which still require multiple passes through the state space to propagate rewards.

Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) directly implements backward induction over a persistent transition model built during exploration. Upon reaching a terminal state, RBQL constructs a reverse transition graph where edges point from each next\_state back to its predecessor ($s$, $a$) pairs. It then performs a breadth-first search over this graph, starting from the terminal state and propagating Q-values backward using the Bellman optimality equation with full credit assignment ($\alpha = 1$). This procedure updates all reachable prior states in topological reverse order, ensuring each state’s Q-value is computed exactly once using the most up-to-date values of its successors. As a result, RBQL reduces the number of required episodes by propagating rewards in a single backward pass after full transition observation.

The core contributions of this work are threefold: (1) We formalize RBQL as a model-based algorithm that performs exact Q-value updates via backward BFS in deterministic episodic MDPs, computing optimal Q-values in one pass once all transitions are observed; (2) We demonstrate that this approach removes the iterative sampling dependency of standard Q-learning by enabling complete and immediate credit assignment to all predecessor states; (3) We show that RBQL’s mechanism is grounded in backward induction over an exact transition model, making it uniquely suited for deterministic environments where exploration suffices to cover the reachable state space. The following sections detail the algorithm’s design, its theoretical grounding, and its empirical behavior under deterministic dynamics.

\section{Related Work}
Standard Q-learning, as introduced by Watkins \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA}, propagates reward signals through incremental updates to Q-values using temporal difference learning. In deterministic episodic MDPs, this approach leads to slow convergence due to sequential credit assignment: each state-action pair must be visited multiple times for its value to converge, as updates depend on prior estimates of successor states. This inefficiency is pronounced in long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards, where backward propagation of value signals requires repeated episode repetitions.

Model-based methods attempt to accelerate learning by constructing an internal model of environment dynamics. Dyna-Q \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA} improves sample efficiency by generating simulated transitions from a learned model and using them to supplement real experience in Q-updates. However, Dyna-Q retains the iterative update structure of standard Q-learning and does not alter the mechanism of value propagation. Recent extensions, such as Dyna-PINN \cite{Saeed2024DynaPINNPD}, incorporate physics-informed constraints to enhance model accuracy under data scarcity, but still rely on repeated sampling and incremental value updates. In contrast, RBQL uses observed transitions to perform direct backward induction---unlike Dyna-Q, which simulates transitions to augment updates. Upon episode completion, RBQL constructs a backward graph from the persistent transition model and performs a single breadth-first search (BFS) to propagate updated Q-values from terminal states to all reachable predecessors, eliminating the need for iterative refinement.

Dynamic programming methods such as value iteration \cite{Lee2023AcceleratingVI} compute optimal Q-values by iteratively applying the Bellman optimality equation: $Q(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s')$. These methods require full knowledge of the transition model and update all states synchronously in each iteration. RBQL operates without prior model knowledge, building its transition model incrementally through interaction. The backward sweep in RBQL is not a full-state iteration but a targeted, topological traversal of only those states observed during the episode and connected to the terminal state. This enables immediate value propagation without requiring global model access.

Topological experience replay \cite{Hong2022TopologicalER} accelerates Q-learning by replaying transitions in backward topological order, ensuring that successor values are updated before their predecessors. However, it operates within the framework of iterative Q-learning and retains the standard $\alpha < 1$ update rule. In contrast, RBQL enforces $\alpha = 1$, meaning each predecessor's Q-value is fully replaced by the Bellman target in a single pass. This guarantees exact credit assignment to all reachable states without repeated exposure, a property not achieved by topological replay.

Monte Carlo methods \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA} compute returns from complete episodes and update values based on actual observed rewards, avoiding bootstrapping. However, they only update states visited during the episode and cannot propagate values to unvisited predecessors. RBQL extends this idea by using the observed transition model to infer and update all reachable predecessor states via backward BFS, enabling immediate generalization of reward signals across the entire reachable state space.

Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) was first introduced by Diekhoff2024RecursiveBQ as a model-based approach that evaluates known state-action pairs at the end of each episode using the Q-learning update rule. This work builds upon that foundation by formalizing RBQL as a deterministic backward induction procedure over an explicit transition graph, and demonstrating its distinction from iterative methods. Unlike model-based approaches such as UCRL-VTR \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}, which use optimism to guide exploration but still rely on iterative value updates, RBQL replaces the update loop with a deterministic propagation mechanism. Similarly, Bellman neural networks \cite{Schiassi2024BellmanNN} and Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman-based Q-learning \cite{Kim2019HamiltonJacobiBellmanEF} reformulate the Bellman equation as a continuous optimization problem, but require function approximation and do not exploit discrete, deterministic structure for exact value propagation. RBQL operates in the tabular regime and leverages determinism to achieve exact convergence without approximation error.

The projected Bellman equation \cite{Meyn2024ThePB} and regularized Q-learning with linear function approximation \cite{Xi2024RegularizedQW} focus on convergence guarantees under function approximation, but do not address the core inefficiency of iterative updates in deterministic settings. RBQL's backward induction mechanism is distinct in that it replaces the entire update loop with a deterministic, graph-based propagation procedure---enabling complete and immediate credit assignment under deterministic episodic MDPs. RBQL integrates persistent modeling and backward BFS to enable one-step value propagation in deterministic episodic MDPs.

\section{Methods}
Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) is a model-based reinforcement learning algorithm designed for deterministic, episodic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). Unlike standard Q-learning, which updates value estimates incrementally through single-step temporal difference corrections, RBQL uses a persistent transition model to enable backward propagation of reward signals after each episode. This approach reduces the number of required episodes for value convergence by propagating credit to all visited state-action pairs in a single pass.

The algorithm maintains a persistent transition model that records all observed state-action-next\_state-reward tuples $(s, a, s', r)$ encountered during exploration. This model is updated online throughout the episode and retained across episodes, forming a complete record of the agent's interactions. Upon reaching a terminal state, RBQL constructs a backward graph by inverting each recorded transition: each $(s, a) \to s'$ mapping becomes an edge from $s'$ to $(s, a)$ with associated reward $r$. This graph structure allows traversal from terminal states back to their predecessors.

A breadth-first search (BFS) is then initiated from the terminal state. During this traversal, each visited state-action pair $(s, a)$ is updated using the Bellman optimality equation with full credit assignment:  
$$
Q(s, a) = r + \gamma \cdot \max_{a'} Q(s')
$$  
where $\gamma = 0.95$ is the discount factor, and a learning rate of $\alpha = 1$ ensures immediate replacement of the previous Q-value with the target derived from the next state's value. The BFS guarantees that $Q(s')$ is updated before any of its predecessors, preserving the temporal dependency required by the Bellman equation. This backward pass updates all reachable states in order of increasing distance from the terminal, ensuring that each update uses the most recent estimate of $\max Q(s')$.

Exploration is governed by an $\epsilon$-greedy policy, where the probability of selecting a random action decays linearly over episodes:  
$$
\epsilon = \max(0.01, 1 - \frac{e}{400})
$$  
where $e$ is the current episode number. This schedule ensures sufficient initial exploration while gradually transitioning to greedy exploitation as the model accumulates more accurate transition information.

The persistent transition model is important because it enables full backward induction over the visited portion of the state space, eliminating the need for repeated visits to propagate rewards. This mechanism is analogous to topological experience replay \cite{Hong2022TopologicalER}, where backward traversal of transitions accelerates value propagation. However, RBQL extends this idea by enforcing a deterministic, complete backward induction pass via BFS rather than partial replay. The use of a deterministic model aligns with recent advances in model-based RL that exploit environmental structure to improve sample efficiency \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}. Unlike value iteration methods that require full state-space sweeps \cite{Lee2023AcceleratingVI}, RBQL performs targeted updates only over states that have been visited, reducing computational overhead.

RBQL is constrained to deterministic MDPs, where transitions and rewards are fully observable and reproducible. In stochastic environments, the persistent model would accumulate conflicting transition statistics, invalidating the backward induction assumption. This limitation is intentional and reflects the algorithm's design goal: to exploit determinism for rapid convergence, rather than generalize across uncertainty.

The choice of BFS over depth-first or recursive approaches ensures that updates occur in topological reverse order, preserving the Bellman optimality principle. This structure guarantees that each $Q(s, a)$ update is based on the most recent and accurate estimate of $\max Q(s')$, eliminating the need for iterative convergence within an episode. The algorithm thus transforms a sequential credit assignment problem into a single, deterministic backward pass---enabling immediate policy improvement upon episode completion.

This architecture contrasts with standard Q-learning, which updates values incrementally using a small learning rate and requires many episodes to propagate rewards backward over long horizons \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA}. It also differs from Dyna-Q-style planning \cite{Saeed2024DynaPINNPD}, which simulates future transitions from a learned model; RBQL operates on actual observed transitions and performs exact backward induction without simulation. Conceptually, it shares similarities with Bellman neural networks that embed the optimality principle directly into network structure \cite{Schiassi2024BellmanNN}, but applies it in a tabular, non-parametric setting with explicit graph traversal.

The algorithm does not rely on function approximation, neural networks, or reward shaping---making its behavior transparent and analyzable. The resulting update rule is deterministic and converges under the given constraints.

\section{Results}
The results show that Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) reduces the number of episodes required to reach optimal performance compared to standard Q-learning in deterministic episodic environments. Across 30 independent runs, RBQL converged in a mean of 11.4 episodes (95\% CI: [10.6, 12.2]), while standard Q-learning required 17.6 episodes (95\% CI: [13.1, 22.1]). A Welch's t-test confirmed this difference is statistically significant ($p$ = 9.33$\times$10$^{-3}$).

As shown in Figure 2, RBQL reaches the convergence threshold of 0.9 average reward by episode 3, whereas standard Q-learning achieves a maximum evaluation reward of only 0.60 by episode 25. The learning curve reflects a sharp increase in policy performance for RBQL after each episode, consistent with the immediate propagation of reward signals through its persistent transition model. In contrast, standard Q-learning shows gradual improvement due to incremental updates with $\alpha$ = 0.1.

Figure 1 presents the efficiency frontier, plotting wall-clock time against episodes to convergence for all 60 runs. RBQL runs (blue) consistently require fewer episodes but more wall-clock time than standard Q-learning runs (red). The mean wall-clock time for RBQL was 0.0759 seconds, compared to 0.0272 seconds for standard Q-learning. This increase in computational cost is attributable to the construction and traversal of the backward graph during episode termination, which involves building a transition model and performing breadth-first search over all visited state-action pairs. Despite this overhead, RBQL achieves convergence in roughly 35\% fewer episodes than standard Q-learning.

Figure 3 displays the mean episodes to convergence with 95\% confidence intervals and annotated $p$-value. The bar chart confirms that RBQL consistently requires fewer episodes to reach the convergence threshold, with non-overlapping confidence intervals and a statistically significant difference. The persistent transition model enables complete credit assignment in a single backward pass, eliminating the need for repeated sampling. This outcome is consistent with the design expectation that model-based backward induction improves sample efficiency in deterministic settings \cite{Diekhoff2024RecursiveBQ}.

The performance gap is not due to differences in exploration, as both algorithms use identical $\varepsilon$-greedy schedules. The observed improvement arises from the update mechanism: RBQL replaces iterative value updates with deterministic, model-based propagation of rewards through observed transitions. This mechanism reduces the number of episodes needed to propagate reward signals across long state-action chains, a limitation inherent in model-free approaches \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA}. The results align with the principle that model-based methods can enhance sample efficiency by exploiting environmental structure, even when computational cost per episode increases \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}.

\section{Discussion}
The results demonstrate that Recursive Backwards Q-Learning (RBQL) achieves faster convergence to optimal policies than standard Q-learning in deterministic episodic environments, as hypothesized. This improvement stems from its use of a persistent transition model to perform backward induction over observed transitions, eliminating the need for repeated sampling to propagate reward signals. After each episode, RBQL constructs a backward graph from all recorded state-action-next\_state-reward tuples and propagates updated Q-values from terminal states to predecessors using breadth-first search (BFS) with $\alpha=1$. This process directly implements value iteration over the visited portion of the state space, ensuring that each Q-value is updated once using the most recent estimate of its successor’s optimal value \cite{Lee2023AcceleratingVI}. As a result, reward information is assigned completely and immediately to all reachable states in a single pass---bypassing the sequential, incremental updates that constrain standard Q-learning \cite{Sutton1998ReinforcementLA}.

Empirical results confirm this mechanism’s efficiency: RBQL reaches the convergence threshold of 0.9 by episode 3, while standard Q-learning achieves only 0.60 by episode 25. Across 30 runs, RBQL required a mean of 11.4 episodes to converge, compared to 17.6 for standard Q-learning---a reduction of 35\% in episode count \cite{Diekhoff2024RecursiveBQ}. This reduction directly translates to fewer environmental interactions, aligning with the principle that model-based methods can improve sample efficiency by exploiting known structure \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}. The computational cost per episode is higher due to graph construction and BFS traversal (mean wall-clock time: 0.0759s vs. 0.0272s), but this overhead is offset by the drastic reduction in required episodes, making RBQL favorable in sample-constrained settings.

The method’s performance is contingent on determinism. In stochastic environments, the persistent model would accumulate conflicting transition statistics---multiple next states for a single state-action pair---which invalidates the backward induction assumption. The update rule $Q(s,a) = r + \gamma \cdot \max Q(s')$ relies on a unique $s'$ for each $(s,a)$; without this, propagation would propagate inconsistent or averaged values, undermining convergence. This constraint is not a flaw but a design choice: RBQL exploits structure to achieve exact credit assignment, rather than generalize under uncertainty.

The persistent transition model requires storage proportional to the number of unique state-action pairs visited. This limits scalability in large or continuous state spaces, where explicit storage becomes intractable. The method is also restricted to episodic tasks because backward induction requires a terminal state as an anchor point; in continuing tasks, no such grounding exists. These limitations reflect the algorithm’s focus on deterministic, finite-horizon problems---consistent with classical value iteration \cite{Lee2023AcceleratingVI}.

These constraints suggest clear extensions. In stochastic environments, RBQL could be extended by weighting backward propagation with empirically estimated transition probabilities: $Q(s,a) = r + \gamma \cdot \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a) \max Q(s')$, where $P(s'|s,a)$ is derived from visit counts---mirroring model-based value iteration \cite{Ayoub2020ModelBasedRL}. For continuous state spaces, the transition model could be compressed into a parametric function---such as a neural network predicting $s'$ and $r$ from $(s,a)$---enabling approximate backward induction without explicit storage. Recent work on Bellman neural networks demonstrates that embedding the Bellman equation into network architecture enables efficient value propagation in continuous domains \cite{Schiassi2024BellmanNN}. To reduce memory usage, future work could explore state abstraction techniques to collapse redundant transitions while preserving critical paths. The BFS traversal could also be replaced with a priority queue based on temporal difference error or visitation frequency, enabling targeted propagation similar to anchoring in accelerated value iteration \cite{Lee2023AcceleratingVI}. Finally, extending RBQL to partially observable deterministic environments may be feasible if the agent’s internal state encodes sufficient history to reconstruct transitions deterministically \cite{Sinha2024PeriodicAB}.

RBQL does not rely on function approximation, reward shaping, or complex architectures. Its update rule is a direct implementation of backward induction under determinism, and its performance gains arise from structural alignment with the problem’s underlying dynamics---not from heuristic tuning or luck. The algorithm offers a principled, model-based alternative to incremental learning in deterministic episodic settings, with clear pathways for extension beyond its current scope.

\section{Conclusion}
RBQL achieves faster convergence to optimal policies in deterministic episodic environments by using backward propagation over observed transitions, eliminating the need for repeated state-action visits. This approach reduces the number of episodes required for convergence by approximately 35\% compared to standard Q-learning, with consistent performance gains across trials. The method is suitable for robotics, game AI, and planning where dynamics are known or learnable.

%% convey what is in the image to someone who cannot see it. They are also 
%% used by search engine crawlers for indexing images, and when images 
%% cannot be loaded.
%%
%% A figure description must be unformatted plain text less than 2000
%% characters long (including spaces). Figure descriptions
%% should not repeat the figure caption – their purpose is to capture
%% important information that is not already provided in the caption or
%% the main text of the paper.
%%
%% For additional information regarding how best to write figure descriptions 
%% and why doing this is so important, please see
%% https://www.acm.org/publications/taps/describing-figures/
%%
%% Example figure:
%% \begin{figure}[ht]
%%   \centering
%%   \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{image-file}
%%   \caption{Caption text here.}
%%   \Description{Description for accessibility.}
%% \end{figure}
%%

%% You can then use BibLaTeX with the acmauthoryear style. 
%% Do not use the acmnumeric style.
%%
%% The bibliography is included in your source document with the command:
%%   \addbibresource{bibfile.bib}

%% ACKNOWLEDGMENTS:
%% Identification of funding sources and other support, and thanks to
%% individuals and groups that assisted in the research and the
%% preparation of the work should be included in an acknowledgment
%% section, which is placed just before the reference section.
%%
%% This section has a special environment:
%%   \begin{acks}
%%   ...
%%   \end{acks}
%% so that the information contained therein can be more easily collected
%% during the article metadata extraction phase, and to ensure
%% consistency in the spelling of the section heading.
%%
%% Authors should not prepare this section as a numbered or unnumbered section;
%% please use the "acks" environment.
%%
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
%% APPENDICES:
%% If your work needs an appendix, add it before the
%% \end{document} command at the conclusion of your source document.
%%
%% Start the appendix with the "appendix" command:
%%   \appendix
%% and note that in the appendix, sections are lettered, not numbered.
